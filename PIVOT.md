# Loop — Engagement Model (the pivot)

*2026-09-23 · the current direction. Loop facilitates recurring participation
in campus life (studying, clubs, events) and measures student engagement as
the byproduct. The measurement is the product; facilitation is how you earn
the right to measure. Supersedes the advising/belonging-signal framing in
`BMC-v2.md`; keeps that file's research intact.*

## Thesis

> Loop is the crew-and-commitment layer on top of campus life. It gives
> students a small recurring group — and a reason to show up — for the things
> that make campus worth being at: study, clubs, events. In doing so it
> produces the one thing student-affairs offices measure and can't get:
> a continuous, behavioral read on who is actually engaged.

**Two-line pitch by audience:**
- To the student: *"The people you show up with — for the stuff you already
  meant to do."*
- To the institution: *"A live engagement signal, earned by running the
  participation, not by surveying it."*

## The reframe: engagement measurement is the product

The "data exhaust" instinct was right — but its only legitimate home is this:
**you don't sell student data to outsiders; you facilitate engagement and hand
the institution the measurement of its own engagement goal.** Facilitation
earns the right to measure. (This is exactly Stellic's move: sell the
workflow, harvest the signal.)

**What Loop measures that nobody else can.** Today's engagement measurement is
poor:
- NSSE — annual, self-report, lagging, low response.
- CampusGroups — only official events, only who scanned.
- Card swipes (rec, dining, library) — siloed, not "engagement."

By facilitating the crews, Loop generates a **continuous, behavioral,
per-student, cross-domain** read: who is in a recurring group, how many,
attending (verified by the stake / check-in), across study + clubs + events,
from week one, over time. A **leading** engagement index, not a lagging
survey. This is the first-50-days signal from the old model — now legitimately
sourced, engagement-framed, and sold to a buyer who already measures
engagement.

**Why facilitation earns it.** You can't measure engagement by asking; you
measure it by being the thing students use to engage. The signal is
trustworthy and continuous *because* Loop is woven into how students
participate — which is also why a competitor can't just buy the metric; they'd
have to facilitate the behavior first.

**It compounds (the moat).** Facilitate → engagement happens → the measurement
proves it worked → justifies the spend → fund more facilitation → richer
measurement. The data gets more valuable per activity facilitated. A survey
vendor or a single-office tool can't build this.

## The facilitation layer (what students touch)

Loop is **not** an events list or a club directory — that is the aggregation
graveyard (Agnes, AppDev's abandoned attempts, Big Red Radar; governance and
no-feeds make it unwinnable). Supply already exists. **Loop supplies the two
missing ingredients that make supply get used: a crew to go with, and a
commitment to go.**

The real reason the Tuesday event is empty isn't that nobody knows — it's that
**nobody wants to walk in alone and nobody committed.** Loop's wedge, in one
line: *everyone else lists what's happening; Loop is the only one that
assembles the group and makes them show.*

Three flavors of one engine:
- **Study** → the recurring study crew (academic anchor).
- **Clubs** → the sub-crew that sticks past week two (belonging anchor).
- **Events** → the crew that goes together (vitality anchor).

Events/clubs are **ingested as anchors** (the campus Localist calendar has an
open API); Loop owns the crew and the show-up, never the listing. Discipline:
ingest supply, own the crew and the commitment. Cross that line and it's a
dead events app.

## The engine (mechanics borrowed from Timeleft)

The mechanism, not the dinner. Timeleft proves four things worth stealing:
1. **A stake converts intent into attendance.** A small refundable deposit is
   the anti-flake lever — the answer to Loop's oldest risk (the third
   meeting). A stake needs a commitment *problem*, so aim the engine at things
   students mean to do and don't (study, gym, going to the thing), not at pure
   fun.
2. **A fixed recurring ritual beats ad-hoc scheduling.** A standing slot kills
   coordination cost — the direct fix for "logistical fatigue."
3. **Blind curation is a feature.** No profiles, no browsing, no rejection —
   the algorithm places you. (Loop already had this.)
4. **The activity is an interchangeable pretext.** One engine, many
   activities, many buyers.

Group ~8 seats, fires at quorum 4–5, so a partial show still lands a real
group. Commitment stack (plan prompt, two "seat held" reminders, quorum-gated
social proof, rotating one-line role, guilt-free re-entry) layered on the
stake.

## Buyer & money

Two layers, same account:
- **Facilitation wedge** — the offices that run the activities: campus
  activities / student life, the learning center (study), rec (fitness later).
  Fast, discretionary, under the bid threshold.
- **Engagement-measurement expand** — the office that owns and panics about
  engagement: Dean of Students / VP Student Life / Student Affairs, tied to
  Institutional Research (NSSE, retention analytics) and the provost. This is
  the `ANGLES.md` "campus-wide layer" (F), now framed as an **engagement
  index** rather than a belonging signal.

Why they pay now: "reasons to come to and stay on campus" is a live, C-level
anxiety (empty post-pandemic campuses hurt belonging → retention, auxiliary
revenue, and the residential value proposition). The evidence base is firmer
than the dinner story — student involvement → persistence is established
(Astin, Tinto), not a stretch. Budget sources: student life / auxiliary
(insulated, self-funded), engagement/retention lines, orientation.

Pricing shape: facilitation land $6–15K/office; engagement-measurement
consolidation to the $75–150K provost/student-life band (EAB's range, money
already spent). Optional student stake (refundable) as the anti-flake lever —
behavioral, not the revenue base.

## Why this data play is legal where the network play wasn't

The forbidden version (Handshake-style) sold a student network to third
parties. This version:
- shows the institution *its own* engagement data (institution = FERPA
  custodian, school-official model);
- aggregate, disclosed in one line at onboarding, opt-in, n≥10;
- binary/behavioral facts, no free-text to staff, framed as **engagement**,
  never wellbeing/surveillance;
- never leaves for an outside buyer.

Same guardrails as the privacy research — now consistent with the product
instead of fighting it. The measurement is of participation students *chose*,
in things they wanted; disclosed, it isn't surveillance.

## Honest limits

1. **Loop measures what it facilitates**, not all of campus. Defense:
   recurring small-group participation is the *high-signal* kind of engagement
   (recurring > one-off for retention), so Loop measures the part that
   matters — but it is not a total campus census, and shouldn't be sold as
   one.
2. **Still hostage to the crew forming and holding.** The stake improves the
   odds; nobody has measured it yet.
3. **Engagement → retention is correlational** at the point of sale until a
   clean study exists. Sell engagement/vitality as the near-term outcome;
   earn the retention claim with the pilot.
4. **The aggregation trap is one design decision away.** The day Loop becomes
   "a better events app," the moat is gone.

## Where it sits

Better than every angle in `ANGLES.md` on three axes: the buyer is spending on
this exact problem *now*; the measurement is a compounding, hard-to-copy asset
rather than a copyable feature; and the data-exhaust value the founder kept
reaching for finally has a legal, on-strategy home. It inherits the same
open risk (does the crew survive to a third meeting) and the same first move.

## The first move (unchanged, and cheap)

One semester, one campus, one activity. Facilitate real crews with the stake +
fixed ritual; instrument show-up and the engagement index. Primary question:
do crews reach a third meeting, and does the stake hold them? Secondary: does
a student-life or IR office recognize the engagement read as something they'd
buy? Randomize newly formed anchors to the commitment stack vs a plain
reminder so the attendance lift is causal — the same study buys the evidence,
the baseline, and the first customer.
